## Seminar Abstract

This seminar investigates the static and dynamic impacts of market integration on renewable energy expansion.

**Key Findings:**

- **Theory:** Market integration statically improves allocative efficiency through gains from trade, and dynamically incentivizes the new entry of renewable power plants.
- **Empirical Test:** Using two recent grid expansions in the Chilean electricity market, the research empirically tests these predictions.
- **Bias Correction:** The study demonstrates that standard event study estimation underestimates dynamic benefits if renewable investments occur in anticipation of market integration. A structural model is built to correct for this bias.
- **Results:** Market integration was shown to result in price convergence across regions, increased renewable generation, and decreased generation cost and pollution emissions. Crucially, the analysis shows that a substantial amount of renewable entry would not have occurred without market integration, indicating that ignoring this dynamic effect would substantially understate the benefits of transmission investments.

## Speaker Biography

**Koichiro Ito** is an Associate Professor at the University of Chicago Harris School of Public Policy.

**Education:**

- BA from Kyoto University
- MA from University of British Columbia
- PhD from the University of California, Berkeley

**Experience:**
Prior to joining the University of Chicago, he served as a SIEPR Postdoctoral Fellow at Stanford University and an Assistant Professor at Boston University.

**Research Interests:**
His research focuses on the intersection of environmental and energy economics, industrial organization, and public economics. Specific areas include:

- Consumer response to nonlinear pricing, dynamic pricing, and rebate programs in electricity markets.
- How intrinsic and extrinsic motivation affects economic decisions.
- How firms strategically react to attribute-based regulation (e.g., fuel economy standards).
- Firm responses to dynamic incentives in sequential forward markets in wholesale electricity markets.

His methodology frequently employs randomized field experiments and quasi-experimental designs to address policy-relevant questions in energy and environmental policy.
