# Distributed Effects of Climate Policy: A Machine Learning Approach

**Speaker:** Christopher Knittel
**Affiliation:** Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT)
**Date:** February 22, 2021
**Event Type:** Live virtual seminar (Registration no longer available)

## Seminar Abstract

This seminar utilizes machine learning techniques to estimate Household Carbon Footprints (HCFs) for the average household within each Census tract.

**Key Findings & Analysis:**

- **Carbon Footprint Variation:** Significant variation exists in carbon footprints across income and geography.
  - Income effects are driven by higher footprints related to transportation and consumer products/services.
  - Geographic effects are primarily due to the variable carbon intensity of the electricity grid.
- **Policy Assessment:** The analysis assesses the net effects of various climate policies on U.S. households, focusing on distribution across geography, urbanity, and income groups.
- **Objective:** To improve understanding of potential regressivity, geographic transfers, and rural-urban transfers among climate policy options.
- **Policy Comparison:** The study compares 12 different policy scenarios, including carbon pricing schemes and regulatory standards.
  - **Regulatory Standards:** Tend to be regressive and are, on average, a net cost to low-income households, especially in rural areas.
  - **Carbon Pricing with Dividend:** When accompanied by a dividend, this approach is progressive for urban, rural, and suburban households. Low-income households receive a larger dividend check than they spend on carbon taxes.
- **Mitigation Strategies:**
  - Transfers from the Midwest and Plains to the Coasts can be mitigated by slightly adjusting the dividend (an increase or decrease of less than 8%).
  - Increasing the progressive structure of a policy benefits rural households more on average but increases overall impact heterogeneity within income groups.
  - Reducing transfers between geographic regions and urban-rural households increases the average benefit to low-income households and reduces impact heterogeneity.

**Conclusion:** Policymakers are encouraged to assess and actively control for unwanted transfers between households when designing climate policies.

## Speaker Biography

**Christopher Knittel** is the George P. Shultz Professor of Applied Economics in the Sloan School of Management at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT).

- **Research Focus:** Environmental economics, studying how firms and consumers respond to policies.
- **Leadership:** He directs MIT’s Center for Energy and Environmental Policy Research, a hub for social science research on energy and the environment since the late 1970s. He also co-directs The E2e Project (MIT and UC Berkeley).
- **Academic History:** Joined MIT faculty in 2011; previously taught at UC Davis and Boston University.
- **Education:**
  - B.A. in Economics and Political Science (California State University, Stanislaus, 1994, *summa cum laude*).
  - M.A. in Economics (UC Davis, 1996).
  - Ph.D. in Economics (UC Berkeley, 1999).
- **Professional Roles:** Research Associate at the National Bureau of Economic Research (Productivity, Industrial Organization, and Energy and Environmental Economics groups).
- **Editorial Roles:** Co-editor of the *Journal of Public Economics*; associate editor of the *Journal of Transportation Economics and Policy* and *Journal of Energy Markets*.
