# Environmental Justice, Air Pollution, and Economics in the U.S. Power Sector

**Seminar Details**

- **Title:** Environmental justice and climate, air pollution, and economics of decisions in the U.S. power sector
- **Speaker:** Inês Azevedo
- **Affiliation:** Stanford University
- **Date/Time:** Monday, March 22, 2021 – 06:00 – 07:15 PM (CET)
- **Format:** Live virtual event (Registration was previously required)

## Seminar Abstract: Key Research Findings

This seminar addresses the intersection of electricity generation, air pollution, and climate policy, with a focus on environmental justice in the United States.

**Air Pollution and Health Impacts:**

- Electricity generation is a major contributor to PM2.5 air pollution.
- The study estimates health effects from air pollution across the US, broken down by the seven Regional Transmission Organizations, US state, income level, and race.
- **Disparities Observed:** Health exposures are higher for lower-income populations, but racial disparities are found to be larger than income disparities.
- **Geographical Mismatch:** Significant differences exist between where electricity is generated and where the resulting health consequences from air pollution are experienced; for 36 US states, most health impacts are attributable to emissions originating in other states.

**Modeling Climate and Health Co-Benefits:**

- The presentation discusses the issue of improved air quality and human health, which are often treated as mere "co-benefits" of climate mitigation but are rarely integrated into policy design.
- A model was developed to optimize emissions reduction costs from the U.S. power sector by considering both climate and health benefits.
- **Optimization:** The model determines the best locations for replacing power plants with new wind, solar, or natural gas facilities to meet a CO2 reduction target.
- **Results:**
  - Reducing CO2 by 30% yields estimated annual health benefits of **$21–$68 billion**.
  - Co-optimizing for both climate and health benefits can yield an additional **$9–$36 billion** in health benefits.
  - The total health benefits are found to equal or exceed climate benefits across a wide range of modeling assumptions, emphasizing the necessity of considering health in climate policy design and promoting interstate cooperation for equitable benefits.

## Speaker Biography: Inês M.L. Azevedo

Inês M.L. Azevedo is an Associate Professor in the Department of Energy Resources Engineering at Stanford University. She also serves as a Senior Fellow for the Woods Institute for the Environment and a Fellow for the Precourt Institute for Energy at Stanford University.

**Research Focus:**
Prof. Azevedo’s research centers on transitioning to a sustainable, low-carbon, affordable, and equitable energy system. Her work employs a systems approach, combining engineering/technology analysis with economic and decision science approaches, with a particular focus on distributional effects and equity in energy issues.

**Academic Contributions:**

- She has published over 100 peer-reviewed journal papers.
- She has contributed to several National Research Council reports from the U.S. National Academy of Sciences.
- She serves on the editorial/advisory boards for journals including *Environmental Research Letters*, *Annual Reviews of Environment and Resources*, *Energy Policy*, and *Energy and Climate Change*.
- She is currently participating in the IPCC AR6 as a lead author for the Energy chapter.

**Awards:**

- World Economic Forum’s “Young Scientists under 40” award (2014).
- C3E Women in Clean Energy Research Award (2017).
