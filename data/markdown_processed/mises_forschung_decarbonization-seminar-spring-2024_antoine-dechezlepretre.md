### Seminar Abstract

Disparities in carbon pricing between trade partners create the risk of carbon leakage, where emissions shift from regions with strict regulations to those with more lenient ones. To counter this, the EU introduced the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), which entered a transitional period in October 2023. This mechanism requires EU importers of certain emission-intensive, trade-exposed goods to pay an adjustment fee on embedded emissions at the European border.

This seminar analyzes the potential effects of CBAM on carbon leakage and the performance of affected sectors across the entire value chain. By utilizing multiple micro-level data sources, an input-output model is employed to simulate CBAM's impact on both European and non-EU industries, factoring in input substitution, price elasticities, and quantity changes.

**Key Findings:**

- The current CBAM regulation covers a small fraction of global emissions and trade (0.18% of global emissions and 0.5% of global trade in intermediary goods and services).
- The introduction of CBAM, combined with the removal of free allowances, has a modest impact on EU and non-EU value-added. In the EU, the removal of free allowances alone would cause a marginal 0.48% reduction in value added, but CBAM mitigates this, resulting in a net effect of -0.30%.
- Redistributing revenues from free allowance removal and CBAM would further lessen the overall impact on EU value-added.
- Outside the EU, the impact is nearly negligible across all industries, though variations exist by country.
- Regarding carbon emissions, CBAM coupled with free allowance reduction leads to a 0.05% decrease in global emissions, effectively mitigating carbon leakage and suggesting modest emission reductions outside the EU. An extended CBAM product list slightly reduces global emissions (-0.01%).
- These findings remain robust across various sensitivity analyses, including alternative carbon prices and trade elasticities.
