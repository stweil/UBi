# The Signal-to-Noise Ratio Hypothesis: A Computational Perspective on General Intelligence

**Speaker:** Anna-Lena Schubert
**Date:** 2 July 2025
**Time:** 14:00
**Source:** [University of Mannheim Summer School Keynote](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/smip-summerschool/keynotes/anna-lena-schubert/)

## Core Hypothesis: SNR as a Global Determinant of Intelligence

The positive manifold underlying general intelligence suggests a common underlying cause—a feature of the cognitive system that influences performance globally, regardless of the specific task or type of information processed.

Anna-Lena Schubert argues that the **Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)** of cognitive processing serves as this global determinant of individual differences.

- **Function of SNR:** SNR governs the fidelity of information processing across all cognitive operations.
- **Implication:** A stable, system-wide SNR is expected to impact performance across diverse cognitive tasks, making it a strong candidate for explaining the general factor of intelligence ($g$).

## Computational Modeling Approach

In computational models of cognition, individual performance differences typically arise from variations in model parameters. A global characteristic like SNR would manifest as a parameter common to models of various cognitive activities.

By comparing models of:

- Simple decisions
- Sensory discrimination
- Categorization
- Decision-making
- Learning
- Working memory

A shared feature emerges: a parameter linked to SNR, which can manifest either as variable signal strength against a fixed noise baseline or as a noise parameter varying across individuals or tasks.

## Evidence and Methodology

The presentation will present initial evidence supporting the SNR hypothesis, focusing on:

1. **Evidence Accumulation Models:** Highlighting correlations between SNR parameters and fluid intelligence in decision-making models.
1. **Working Memory Models:** Demonstrating similar correlations in working memory studies.
1. **Advanced Modeling:** Showing how a neurocognitive computational modeling approach can address identifiability challenges, allowing for the estimation of signal and noise as distinct, interpretable parameters.

This framework offers a promising path to deepen the understanding of the cognitive and computational basis of intelligence.
