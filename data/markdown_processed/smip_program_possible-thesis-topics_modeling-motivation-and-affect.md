# Modeling Motivation and Affect: Research Focus and Thesis Topics

Affective states (e.g., feeling happy, relaxed, or fatigued) fluctuate significantly due to factors like diurnal rhythms, daily events, and self-regulatory behavior. Modern research increasingly treats constructs originally viewed as individual differences (like work engagement or personality traits) as dynamic states that vary moment-to-moment or day-to-day.

The core challenge in this field is understanding the interconnected dynamics between momentary affect, other experiences, and behavior over time, including identifying their predictors and consequences.

## Methodological Context: Intensive Longitudinal Data (ILD)

To gain insight into these complex psychological processes, researchers are collecting **Intensive Longitudinal Data (ILD)** using modern technology (e.g., smartphones, sensors). ILD possess a complex structure, necessitating advanced statistical modeling techniques that can effectively disentangle within-person variance from between-person variance.

- **Primary Method:** Hierarchical models (multilevel models) are typically employed for ILD analysis.
- **Advanced Approaches:** To address specific research questions regarding within-person dynamics (and associated between-person differences), combining different modeling frameworks (e.g., multilevel and latent-class analytic approaches) or applying methods from experimental psychology can be highly beneficial.

## Dissertation Project Scope

Supervision covers two main types of projects:

1. Dissertation projects addressing substantive research questions on affective-motivational processes using advanced statistical modeling.
1. Dissertation projects focused more directly on model development.

Research questions generally cover:

- Affective experiences and affect regulation.
- Work-related processes (e.g., job stressor perceptions, recovery from work).
- Properties of assessment methods (e.g., compliance in ambulatory assessment, assessment of sensitive attributes).

*(Note: Ph.D. students are welcome to devise their own thesis topic in consultation with the primary SMiP advisor during the first semester.)*
