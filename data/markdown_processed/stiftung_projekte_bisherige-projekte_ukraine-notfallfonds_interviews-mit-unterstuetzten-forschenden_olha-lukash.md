# Olha Lukash: Research and Resilience from Ukraine

[Olha Lukash](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/stiftung/projekte/bisherige-projekte/ukraine-notfallfonds/interviews-mit-unterstuetzten-forschenden/olha-lukash/) is an Associate Professor at the Faculty of Economics, Entrepreneurship, and Business Administration at the State University of Sumy. Her research focus is environmental economics. She arrived in Mannheim with her daughter in February 2023 and currently works at the Chair of Quantitative Economics.

## Life and Displacement During the War in Ukraine

**Q: How did you and your family experience the situation in Sumy?**
Sumy is located in the northeastern part of Ukraine, only 40 kilometers from the Russian border. It is a beautiful city with many parks, rivers, and three universities, but everything is different now because of the war; no one can feel safe. Sumy was among the first cities affected by the Russian aggression. It was terrible. Suddenly, there was a feeling of uncertainty, horror, and insecurity. My first thought was: How can I get my daughter to safety?

**Q: When did you decide to leave Ukraine?**
We initially couldn't leave Sumy because it was already occupied. It is hard to remember that time because we lived for a long time in a cold basement that served as our shelter. There were constant bombings and artillery shelling. Furthermore, there was a shortage of food and medicine, and the electricity and water supply were cut off. We had no heating, no food, and on some days, no mobile connection, so we couldn't even call our relatives to let them know how we were doing.

**Q: When were you finally able to leave Sumy?**
We debated every day how to get to a safer place. But we knew it was dangerous. Some families attempted to flee, but many families died during that attempt. When we finally left Sumy, we passed many burnt-out cars. I cannot tell you what terrible things we saw on the streets—cars with bullet holes. Many of them had stickers with children's names. We left the city in mid-March 2022, when the green corridors were established, and stayed in Poltava for a month. However, when the Russian army attacked the trade center in Kremenchuk in the Poltava region, it became clear to us that nowhere in Ukraine was safe. In May, we returned to Sumy and began looking for ways to leave the country and find a place where I could work and receive support.

**Q: Why did you decide to come to Germany?**
I had been to Germany many times, mainly for conferences, seminars, and summer schools. I like Germany very much—the people, the traditions—and it was my dream to come here one day. So, when I saw the advertisement from the University of Mannheim Foundation for researchers affected by the war in Ukraine, I applied for a scholarship and was thrilled when I received positive feedback. It is a great opportunity for me, and a happy coincidence that I can collaborate with Professor Ulrich Wagner from the Faculty of Economics. His research is very close to my area of interest.

## Academic Focus and Challenges

**Q: How was your start in Germany?**
I received a lot of support from the University of Mannheim Foundation, as well as from Katharina Bolle, who supports researchers here at the university. However, I noticed significant differences between Ukraine and Germany, especially regarding bureaucracy. If you want to open a bank account and get a card in Ukraine, it is done in 30 minutes. Here, I had to make an appointment and wait for a week to receive letters, cards, and secret numbers.

**Q: Can you tell us about your research field?**
For my doctoral thesis, I studied the environmental aspects of cross-border cooperation. However, I have shifted my focus to methods for reducing carbon dioxide emissions in Ukraine. My goal is to develop practical and theoretical assessments of how these decarbonization processes affect climate change, economic development, regional sustainability, and other related factors. The results of this research could be implemented by politicians, companies, and households to manage the transition to clean energy while considering economic, social, and ecological aspects.

**Q: What measures do you find most promising for decarbonization in Ukraine?**
Unfortunately, the situation in Ukraine has changed drastically due to the ongoing war. Before the war, the Ukrainian government had developed a roadmap for CO2 reduction in line with international agreements and to mitigate the effects of climate change. However, the focus has shifted significantly due to the war. A large part of the country's infrastructure is destroyed; houses have been bombed, and vital supply systems need to be restored, which means we have to rebuild the country. These efforts inevitably cause additional emissions, so we must adapt our clean energy roadmap.

**Q: Do you think current measures are sufficient to tackle the climate crisis?**
It is a complex topic because there is often a conflict between economic and ecological interests. Many companies are reluctant to recognize these problems and take measures to change. In Ukraine, we face challenges due to the old coal facilities and the need to consider the employment of mining workers during the transition to clean energy. This presents us with economic difficulties, but similar problems exist in other countries, such as Poland. We must develop regulatory measures to offer incentives for this change to companies.

**Q: How does the war affect the ecosystems in Ukraine?**
Before the war, there were significant efforts to invest in solar power plants in Ukraine, and the number of solar installations increased sharply. However, the war has led to the destruction of large parts of this infrastructure. It is heartbreaking to see how all the efforts for clean energy are undone in an instant. Nature also suffers: I read a study about the destructive impact on ecosystems, and I just wanted to cry. There are reports, for example, of dolphins killed by mines in the Black Sea. The war has not only destroyed infrastructure and human lives but has also severely damaged the ecological environment.

**Q: That is terrible news. Can anything be done about it?**
Peace is the only solution. I hope the situation improves soon and that peace returns, but I don't know how we will achieve that. So many people have lost their friends, families, and homes. I just want us to resume our normal lives and not be confronted with problems caused by war. However, I also want to express my gratitude to the University of Mannheim and the Foundation for giving me and my family the opportunity to come here. As the saying goes: there are no bad things without something good.
